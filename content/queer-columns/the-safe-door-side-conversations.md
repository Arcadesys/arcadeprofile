## The bargain and the other map

What do you do when the official door stays closed?

Black Americans have argued over this bargain for more than a century, and their argument is the clearest map I know.

In 1895, [Booker T. Washington stood before a largely white audience in Atlanta](https://www.nps.gov/bowa/learn/historyculture/atlanta1-1.htm) and offered a deal. Black Southerners would set aside the fight for social equality for now. They would focus on trades, land, and business. Build wealth first; rights would follow. His critics called it the Atlanta Compromise.

[W.E.B. Du Bois answered in 1903](https://etc.usf.edu/lit2go/203/the-souls-of-black-folk/4434/chapter-3-of-mr-booker-t-washington-and-others). In _The Souls of Black Folk_, he argued that Washington was asking Black Americans to give up three things at once: political power, the fight for civil rights, and higher education for their young people. Those were the very things that would protect whatever they built.

History backed him up in the ugliest way. In 1921, [a white mob burned the Greenwood district of Tulsa](https://www.nps.gov/places/oklahoma-john-hope-franklin-reconciliation-park.htm), one of the most prosperous Black communities in the country. The shops were real. The homes were real. The wealth was real. Without the power to protect it, none of that mattered.

By the 1960s, [Malcolm X was asking a different question](https://www.malcolm-x.org/docs/gen_oaau.htm). Why keep asking for a seat in institutions built to exclude you? Build your own. Protect your own.

I won't pretend the histories match. Slavery, Jim Crow, and lynching have no equivalent in my story. And Black trans women have lived both struggles at once, which is exactly why the comparison matters. But it is the same argument, and every marginalized group ends up having it:

_How much should you change yourself to survive a hostile society, and how much should you insist that society change instead? And when do you stop asking and build your own?_

Each of those men was right about something. Washington was right that you have to eat while you wait for justice. Du Bois was right that nothing you build is safe without the power to protect it. Malcolm was right that some doors never open, no matter how politely you knock. At its best, the other map was trans people's version of his answer.

## The gender machinery

What does passing teach about rules that claim to be natural?

There is another reason trans people provoke such outsized anxiety.

Gender is more than a description of bodies. It is a social system full of instructions, expectations, rewards, punishments, and contradictions. We teach men and women how to behave, then pretend those lessons came from the body itself.

Men get obvious double binds. Be strong, but emotionally available. Be capable of violence, but never frightening. Protect your family, but don't control them. Know how to dominate a room, but don't become an asshole.

Women get their own impossible maze. Be attractive, but not vain. Be nurturing, but professionally ambitious. Be assertive enough to lead, but not enough to become abrasive. Age gracefully, but preferably without appearing to age.

Much of gender is learning to navigate those contradictions without looking like you're navigating them.

Sociologists Candace West and Don Zimmerman described gender as something people _do_, a "routine accomplishment" produced in everyday interaction rather than a property attached to the body. Their point was not that bodies are imaginary. It was that being recognizable as a man or a woman takes ongoing work. ([West and Zimmerman, "Doing Gender," 1987](https://doi.org/10.1177/0891243287001002002)) Raewyn Connell's work rejects the idea of a single, natural masculinity: masculinities are multiple, historically contingent, and organized through hierarchies among men. ([Raewyn Connell,](https://www.raewynconnell.net/p/masculinities_20.html) [_Masculinities_](https://www.raewynconnell.net/p/masculinities_20.html))

C. J. Pascoe documented one ugly way that hierarchy holds. In her ethnography _Dude, You're a Fag_, adolescent boys disciplined one another's masculinity through feminizing and homophobic insults. The point was not to identify anyone as gay. The insult was a warning: you are failing at masculinity. Get back in line. ([University of California Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/dude-youre-a-fag/paper))

Julia Serano, in _Whipping Girl_, calls the belief underneath all this _oppositional sexism_: men and women are supposed to be opposites, so whatever one has, the other must lack. ([Julia Serano, "What Is Transmisogyny?"](https://juliaserano.medium.com/what-is-transmisogyny-4de92002caf6)) The hierarchy needs the opposition. "Men are like this" only works if women are something else.

That is the racket: it works best when everybody believes the rules are destiny.

And then trans people wander into the machinery.

Not as revolutionaries. Often we're trying to do the opposite. Plenty of us, myself included, have spent years learning to fit inside one of those categories.

I learned the rules. That doesn't mean my womanhood is fake. It means the rules were learnable.

## Where the shame comes from

What happens when those rules become visible?

That is where I think the shame comes from.

Nearly everyone has been wounded somewhere by the gender bargain. Boys who learned young that terror was preferable to tears. Men who reached adulthood without the vocabulary to explain loneliness. Girls who learned that being liked was safer than being angry. Women who mastered the art of doing twice as much work while appearing effortlessly pleasant.

Most people adapt. They find versions of masculinity or femininity they can inhabit. Sometimes they find joy there. Sometimes they carry resentment. Usually both.

But the system has an easier time sustaining itself if those compromises feel inevitable. If men suffer because _that's what being a man is_, there is nothing to interrogate. If women suffer because _that's just how women are treated_, perhaps there is nothing underneath to dismantle.

Once gender becomes visible as a system of incentives and sanctions, harder questions appear.

Why did we teach that boy that tenderness made him weak? Why was that woman's anger treated differently from a man's? Why do we assign moral meaning to a skirt, a haircut, a voice, a gesture, a pronoun? And if somebody can cross the boundary permanently and live perfectly well on the other side, what exactly was holding the boundary up?

---

What do I take from this?

When a culture is anxious about masculinity, femininity, sex, family, or power, trans people become convenient objects for that anxiety. We are visible enough to symbolize the instability without having caused it.

If the system depends on male and female being natural opposites, then somebody who moves between the categories, combines traits from each, or declines them altogether looks less like ordinary human variation and more like a contradiction that needs hammered into place.

## The three-article rule

How could a rule work if nobody could find it in the statute book?

The so-called *three-article rule* is a revealing part of this record. People remember police demanding three items of clothing associated with the sex assigned to them at birth. [Vicki St. James recounts being made to undress beside an Indiana highway](https://www.pbs.org/video/the-queens-kings-of-midwest-drag-gm8aip/) to prove she had them. The same PBS film explains the catch: researchers have found no written three-article law. Police still used the rule to humiliate people. [Historian Kate Redburn's search through statutes and police manuals](https://www.history.com/articles/stonewall-riots-lgbtq-drag-three-article-rule) found no three-item requirement either.
